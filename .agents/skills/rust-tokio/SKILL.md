# Rust Tokio

**Ownership.** Identify who admits work, owns its actual execution, observes failure, requests cancellation, and waits for completion. Follow those responsibilities across future, task, thread, and resource boundaries; the caller's wait and the operation's lifetime need not coincide. Honor supplied requirements and preserve settled choices outside the requested change; resolve only what the task leaves open.

Observe meaningful task outcomes while their parent is serving, not only during shutdown. Distinguish unexpected completion from a requested drain; a join winning a select is not automatically a crash. Dropping a spawn handle detaches its task and loses the returned result. In this service, [runtime lifecycle](../../../docs/architecture/runtime-lifecycle.md) owns admission, Background registration, root cancellation, and joining. A standalone program assigns those responsibilities to its existing parent without importing a framework. Cancellation propagation follows the selected primitive's contract; it does not by itself establish that child work or effects have stopped.

At a suspension point, a future may be dropped. Identify which progress, queue position, or owned payload may be discarded and whether that is permitted. Select drops losing futures it owns; a borrowed future can remain with its owner, and pinning alone does not preserve it after that owner is dropped. Check the specific operation: read can be cancellation-safe while read_exact and write_all can leave partial progress. A cancelled pending send may lose its value or queue position without retracting a delivered message. Keep invariants valid across cancellation and preserve data separately when the accepted contract requires it. Biased polling establishes order of polling, not an atomic admission barrier.

Prefer a synchronous mutex for short critical sections with low contention and no await while guarded. When contention or required suspension would block executor progress, consider an async mutex, reduced sharing, or an existing task-owned resource. Keep data whose invariant must change atomically under coherent protection. Before calling a callback or returning guarded access, examine reentry, lock ordering, blocking, and how much lock lifetime the caller would control. Moving a callback out of a lock is valid only if the required atomicity survives. Poison recovery needs project permission and a way to re-establish the protected invariant; this service retains its adopted no-recovery policy. Choose atomic ordering from the synchronization relation it must establish; scrutinize SeqCst without weakening it merely to remove that spelling.

An async wrapper or mutex does not make synchronous I/O nonblocking. Move the complete blocking operation, including its transaction and lock scope, behind the existing blocking owner or a supported async adapter. Keep cheap bounded computation inline. Distinguish sustained CPU work from occasional blocking calls and from loops over immediately-ready futures: an await does not guarantee yielding. Cooperative yielding cannot make a blocking system call nonblocking. Bound work in each poll and arrange a wakeup before returning Pending.

Bound admission before submitting blocking or CPU work, retaining its permit through actual execution and unwinding. A started spawn_blocking closure can outlive handle abort and timeout; waiter cancellation must not release its execution capacity early. Keep an owner observing termination and panic, request cooperative cancellation when supported, and account for residual work at shutdown. The [execution-lifetime example](references/execution-lifetime.md) applies when waiter and execution lifetimes differ. A retry may overlap earlier work and still needs effect and identity rules. Bound queued work through the queue or an established finite admission bound; count retained payloads as well as task count.

Prefer a suitable completion or change notification over a sleep loop. Where external state offers no notification, polling needs an owner, bounded cadence, and terminal condition. A notification that stores no permit requires a race-free state check or another accepted wakeup protocol. Use timeout for cooperative waiting on a future; it cannot preempt non-yielding work, and expiry does not establish non-execution of an external effect. For a hard resource-lifetime promise, identify an owner able to reclaim it even if the consumer stops polling. Trusted synchronous callbacks still require a bounded source and cost; a byte-limited output cannot preempt their computation.

Use Tokio time for code controlled by a paused clock, and choose missed-tick behavior from the operation's semantics. Keep signal streams alive for their intended lifetime. The entry owner selects runtime shutdown behavior: a shutdown timeout stops waiting but may leave blocking execution alive. In this service, use the lifecycle owner's deadlines and cleanup order instead of adding an independent shutdown path. Prefer async fn when its contract is sufficient; explicit Future returns remain useful for required setup or interface behavior.

For review, trace affected ownership, synchronization, cancellation, and completion without editing, and distinguish static evidence from observed progress. For implementation, use the project's focused proof, synchronize on observable state or completion rather than sleeping, bound waits, and leave no task owned by the test running after success. Reuse existing execution machinery; neither a new actor, executor, timer, nor an expanded lifecycle audit is required merely because this method names it.
